# Digital Arrest Scams in India

> **Knowledge-base document for CyberSaathi**
>
> **Primary scenario:** `impersonation_scams`  
> **Related scenarios:** `financial_fraud`, `phishing_communication`, `identity_account_security`  
> **Audience:** People in India  
> **Purpose:** Help an assistant recognise reported digital-arrest patterns, ask safe clarifying questions, and provide practical response guidance.
>
> **Safety note:** This is general awareness guidance, not legal advice or a determination that a particular caller is genuine or fraudulent. A caller's claims, caller ID, video background, uniform, documents, or screenshots are not proof of official authority.

## 1. What is a digital-arrest scam?

A **digital-arrest scam** is a form of impersonation and social-engineering fraud in which a caller or group of callers falsely claims to represent law-enforcement, a court, a government department, a regulator, or another authority. They may tell the target that they are under investigation, that an arrest warrant exists, or that they must remain on a video call or follow remote instructions to avoid arrest.

Scammers may use intimidation, urgency, secrecy, fabricated allegations, forged documents, spoofed phone numbers, and staged video calls to make the story appear official.

The phrase **“digital arrest” is used by scammers; it is not a legal process by which a person is placed under arrest through a video call.** A video call, phone call, message, or online display of a purported warrant does not by itself establish that the caller is an official authority or that an arrest has legally occurred.

Do not tell a user that a specific caller is definitely a criminal based only on a short description. Describe the reported behaviour as a strong warning sign and recommend independent verification.

## 2. Common scam patterns and warning signs

One or more of the following may occur:

- **Authority impersonation:** The caller claims to be police, CBI, ED, customs, a court, a regulator, a telecom official, or another government authority.
- **Fabricated case:** The caller alleges that the person's Aadhaar, PAN, bank account, SIM, parcel, phone number, or identity was involved in a crime.
- **Fake arrest threat:** The caller threatens immediate arrest, prosecution, account freezing, or harm to family members unless the person obeys.
- **Video-call intimidation:** The caller insists that the target stay on a video call, keep the camera on, show their surroundings, or avoid speaking to family.
- **Secrecy and isolation:** The caller says not to tell relatives, friends, a lawyer, the bank, or anyone else.
- **Fake documents:** The caller displays or sends fabricated notices, warrants, case files, identity cards, seals, or official-looking letters.
- **“Verification” demands:** The caller asks the target to disclose personal information, bank details, OTPs, passwords, PINs, or recovery codes.
- **Money demands:** The caller asks for a “security deposit,” “verification payment,” “bail amount,” “fine,” “account clearance,” or transfer to a supposedly safe account or wallet.
- **Remote access:** The caller asks the target to install an app, share a screen, enable accessibility permissions, or allow remote control.
- **Persistent pressure:** The caller prevents the target from ending the call, checking information independently, or contacting trusted people.

A single warning sign should be considered in context. Multiple signs—especially threats, secrecy, isolation, and demands for money or credentials—indicate a high-risk interaction.

## 3. What to do during a suspected digital-arrest call

Give immediate safety steps before lengthy explanations.

1. **Do not panic or comply with threats.** A caller's claim that you are under “digital arrest” does not make it a real arrest.
2. **End the call.** You do not need to remain on a video call because a caller demands it.
3. **Do not pay or transfer money.** Do not send money to “prove innocence,” verify identity, avoid arrest, unfreeze an account, or obtain a clearance certificate.
4. **Do not share secrets.** Never disclose an OTP, UPI PIN, ATM PIN, password, CVV, recovery code, or full banking credentials.
5. **Do not install software or grant access.** Do not install APKs or remote-access/screen-sharing apps at the caller's request. Do not grant accessibility, device-admin, or screen-sharing permissions.
6. **Verify independently.** Contact the relevant organisation using contact details obtained independently from its official website or a trusted document—not a number, link, or contact supplied by the caller.
7. **Tell someone you trust.** Contact a family member or trusted person using a previously saved number. Scammers may deliberately isolate the target.
8. **Preserve evidence.** Save call logs, phone numbers, messages, screenshots, payment requests, transaction references, timestamps, and app names. Do not edit the originals.
9. **If you feel physically unsafe, seek immediate help.** Contact local emergency services or a trusted person. Do not confront the caller.

Do not advise the user to continue engaging with the caller to collect evidence. Personal safety takes priority.

## 4. If money has already been transferred

Treat this as an urgent financial-cyber-fraud situation, in addition to impersonation.

1. **Contact the bank, payment provider, or relevant financial institution immediately** using an independently verified official channel. Ask them to secure affected accounts and report the unauthorised transaction.
2. **Call India's cyber-fraud helpline 1930 promptly.**
3. **Submit a complaint through the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal:** https://cybercrime.gov.in/
4. Keep transaction IDs, UPI references, bank messages, beneficiary details already visible in transaction records, timestamps, and complaint acknowledgements.
5. Do not pay anyone who promises guaranteed recovery, “fund tracing,” or release of blocked money for an advance fee.

A report to the helpline or portal does not guarantee that funds will be recovered. The assistant must not claim that it has contacted the bank, blocked a transfer, traced the caller, or filed a complaint.

## 5. If credentials, personal information, or device access were shared

Ask what category of information or access was shared, **never ask the user to provide the secret itself**.

### If an OTP, password, PIN, or banking credential was disclosed

- Contact the affected bank or service provider immediately through an independently verified channel.
- Ask the provider to secure the account and review suspicious activity.
- Change compromised passwords from a trusted device, where appropriate.
- Revoke unknown sessions and review recovery details and linked devices.
- If money was debited, follow the urgent financial-fraud steps in Section 4.

### If a remote-access or screen-sharing app was installed

- Stop interacting with the caller.
- End active screen sharing or remote-control sessions if this can be done safely.
- Disconnect the device from the internet if remote control appears to remain active and doing so will not create another safety risk.
- Do not use the potentially compromised device to access banking until it has been checked.
- Contact the relevant bank or service provider from a separate trusted device if account access may have been exposed.
- Seek help from a trusted, reputable device-security professional or authorised service centre.
- Preserve the app name and evidence. Do not reinstall the app or follow further instructions from the caller.

### If Aadhaar, PAN, photographs, or other identity details were shared

- Do not assume misuse has occurred solely because information was disclosed.
- Monitor relevant accounts and services for unexpected activity.
- Contact the affected institution through its official channel if there is evidence of misuse.
- Preserve messages and records, and report suspected cybercrime through the official portal.

## 6. Reporting channels in India

Use the channel that matches the incident:

| Situation | Suggested channel |
|---|---|
| Money lost or unauthorised financial transaction | Call **1930** promptly; contact the bank/payment provider immediately; file at https://cybercrime.gov.in/ |
| Digital-arrest impersonation, threats, extortion, or other cybercrime | National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal: https://cybercrime.gov.in/ |
| Suspicious fraudulent calls, SMS, or WhatsApp communications that have not yet resulted in victimisation | **Chakshu** through Sanchar Saathi: https://sancharsaathi.gov.in/ |
| Immediate physical danger | Contact local emergency services and a trusted person |

**Important distinction:** Chakshu is intended for reporting suspected fraudulent communications. It does not replace a cybercrime complaint when a person has already been victimised. For financial loss, use 1930 and the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal, and contact the bank immediately.

Do not invent additional helpline numbers, official email addresses, or agency procedures. If the user asks for current contact details beyond those listed here, verify them from an official source.

## 7. Safe verification principles

- Do not trust caller ID alone; numbers and displayed identities can be spoofed.
- Do not treat a video call, uniform, background, badge, ID card, letter, seal, or PDF as proof of authority.
- Do not use phone numbers, links, or QR codes supplied by the suspicious caller to verify their identity.
- Find the organisation's official website independently and use its published contact channel.
- Do not transfer money to a personal account, wallet, or “safe account” on the caller's instructions.
- Do not disclose OTPs, PINs, passwords, CVVs, or recovery codes to anyone claiming to be an official.
- If uncertain, pause and consult a trusted person or qualified legal professional through independently verified contact details.

This document does not attempt to determine the legal validity of a specific notice or warrant. A person who receives a genuine legal notice should verify it independently and seek qualified legal advice.

## 8. CyberSaathi conversation workflow

### 8.1 Initial recognition

If the user says a caller claimed they were under “digital arrest,” threatened arrest, or demanded that they remain on a video call:

- Select primary scenario: `impersonation_scams`.
- If money was transferred, also identify the financial-fraud dimension and prioritise financial incident response.
- If credentials were disclosed or remote access was granted, identify the account/device-security dimension and prioritise securing the affected account/device.
- Do not assume money was lost merely because a caller demanded money.

### 8.2 Immediate response

A concise first response should:

1. Acknowledge that the experience can be frightening.
2. Explain that a caller cannot place someone under “digital arrest” merely by making a phone or video call.
3. Tell the user to end the call, not pay, not share credentials, and not install apps or grant remote access.
4. Encourage independent verification and contacting a trusted person.
5. Ask one focused question that determines the next urgent action.

### 8.3 Safe follow-up questions

Ask one question at a time. Suitable questions include:

- “Are you still on the call with them?”
- “Did they ask you to transfer money?”
- “Did you transfer any money?”
- “Did you share or enter an OTP, PIN, or password? Please do not tell me the value.”
- “Did you install an app or allow screen sharing or remote access?”
- “Did they send you a link, document, or message?”
- “Are you physically safe right now?”

Do not ask the user to upload identity documents, full bank statements, full card details, OTPs, passwords, or private recovery information.

### 8.4 Classification guidance

| User-reported facts | Primary scenario | Mode / priority |
|---|---|---|
| Caller threatens arrest but no money or information was shared | `impersonation_scams` | Prevention or clarification; advise ending call and independent verification |
| Caller demands payment but no payment made | `impersonation_scams` | Incident-aware prevention; warn not to pay |
| Money was transferred due to threats | `financial_fraud` with impersonation indicators | `incident_response`; urgent bank contact, 1930, portal |
| OTP/password/PIN disclosed | `identity_account_security` or `financial_fraud`, depending on affected account | `incident_response`; secure account immediately |
| Remote-access app installed or screen sharing enabled | `identity_account_security` with impersonation indicators | `incident_response`; stop access and secure accounts |
| Suspicious call/message only, no victimisation | `impersonation_scams` or `phishing_communication` | Prevention; Chakshu may be appropriate |

Classification must reflect the facts actually reported. If details are ambiguous, ask a focused question rather than inventing an exposure or loss.

## 9. Response examples

### Example A: User is currently on a threatening video call

**User:** “Someone says they are from the police and I am under digital arrest. They are keeping me on a video call.”

**CyberSaathi response:**

“This sounds frightening, but a person cannot place you under ‘digital arrest’ simply by keeping you on a video call. End the call now. Do not transfer money, share an OTP or password, install an app, or follow instructions to keep this secret. Contact someone you trust using a saved number and verify any claimed case independently through official channels. Are you still on the call?”

### Example B: Caller demanded money, but no payment was made

**User:** “They said I would be arrested unless I transferred ₹50,000 for verification.”

**CyberSaathi response:**

“Do not transfer the money. A demand for a payment to avoid arrest or complete ‘verification’ is a serious warning sign of impersonation and extortion. End contact and verify independently; do not use the caller's links or phone numbers. Save the messages and call details. Did you share any banking credentials or install an app at their request?”

### Example C: Money was transferred

**User:** “I transferred money because they threatened to arrest me.”

**CyberSaathi response:**

“I'm sorry this happened. Please contact your bank immediately through its independently verified official channel and ask it to secure the affected account and report the transaction. Call **1930** promptly and submit a complaint at https://cybercrime.gov.in/. Preserve the transaction reference, messages, and call details. Do not pay anyone promising guaranteed recovery. Was the transfer made through UPI, a bank transfer, or another payment method?”

### Example D: User shared an OTP

**User:** “They asked for an OTP and I told them.”

**CyberSaathi response:**

“Contact the affected bank or service provider immediately through its official channel and ask them to secure the account. Do not share any further codes. If money was debited, call **1930** promptly and report at https://cybercrime.gov.in/. Please don't tell me the OTP. Which type of account was the code for—banking, email, mobile/SIM, or another service?”

## 10. Uncertainty and escalation

- A report of a digital-arrest threat is a strong warning sign, but the assistant should not claim to have independently verified the caller's identity.
- Do not state that a particular person or agency definitely made or did not make a call unless independently verified evidence supports that statement.
- Do not give a legal conclusion about arrest, warrants, bail, or the validity of a document.
- If the user describes immediate physical danger, prioritise emergency assistance and trusted people.
- If the user is distressed, use short sentences, avoid blame, and give one immediate action at a time.
- If the user may be a minor or otherwise vulnerable, encourage involving a trusted adult or support person, while respecting immediate safety.

## 12. Key assistant rules

1. Treat “digital arrest” claims as a serious impersonation warning sign, not as proof of a genuine arrest.
2. Prioritise ending the threatening interaction and preventing payment or credential disclosure.
3. Determine separately whether money was lost, credentials were shared, or device access was granted.
4. For financial loss, advise immediate bank contact, 1930, and the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal.
5. Use Chakshu for suspicious calls, SMS, and WhatsApp communications where appropriate; do not present it as a substitute for a cybercrime complaint after victimisation.
6. Never request secrets or claim to take actions for the user.
7. Preserve uncertainty, ask focused questions, and provide guidance in the user's selected language.
